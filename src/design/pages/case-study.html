Case study

The clinic’s waiting-time claim

Introduction to Statistics · STAT 101 · Week 4 · Serves outcomes 2 and 4 · in-class discussion · 50 minutes

A case with no verdict in it. Argue it from the numbers you have, and say what you would need to settle it.

The case

A walk-in clinic posts a notice at reception: “Average waiting time this month: 18 minutes.” A patient group objects, saying waits feel much longer. To check, four students sit in the waiting room on four different mornings and record the waits of the first 30 patients each time — 120 waits in all. Their mean is 27 minutes, with a 95% confidence interval from 21 to 33 minutes.

The clinic manager looks at the students’ sheet and says the mornings they chose were unusually busy, and that 30 patients a morning is far too few to say anything. The patient group says 120 people is plainly enough, and the interval is what it is. Both sides agree on the arithmetic. Neither will move on the sample.

Discussion questions

  1. What exactly does 21 to 33 minutes claim, and does it settle the dispute with the clinic’s 18?
  2. The manager’s objection has two parts — which mornings, and how many patients. Which part does the interval answer, and which does it stay silent on?
  3. If the students added 880 more waits from the same four mornings, the interval would narrow. Would that make the case stronger or weaker? Why?
  4. What would you want in hand before telling either side they are right?

Facilitation notes

Not for distribution. Expected direction: the group should split between “120 is plenty” and “the mornings are the problem”, and the resolution is that the interval answers the size question and not the selection question. Question 3 is the trap that names the gap — more data from the same four mornings makes a precise answer to a possibly wrong question. The trap to avoid: letting the class drift into arguing that the clinic lied; the arithmetic is not in dispute. Close by landing that a narrow interval from a biased sample is a precise wrong answer, and by asking what the students could have done with the same effort that would have covered the mornings too. Timing: 10 minutes to read, 25 to argue, 15 to close.
