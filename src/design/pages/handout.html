Reading a confidence interval

Introduction to Statistics · STAT 101 · Week 4 · Serves outcomes 2 and 4 · 2 pages

An interval is a way of saying how much a number would move if you asked a different sample. This page is what to check before you repeat one.

What the number is doing

A sample mean is one draw. Ask a different 36 shops, a different 200 students, a different ten deliveries, and you get a different mean, not because the world changed, but because samples vary. A confidence interval draws the range that this wander would cover if you took the sample again and again.

“95% confident” is short for: intervals built this way capture the true value in 95% of samples. It is a statement about the method, not a promise about this one interval, and not a range that holds 95% of the data.

Three sentences to try

  1. "The average is plausibly between A and B.": always safe, and usually what the reporter meant.
  2. "95% of the values fall between A and B.": rarely what an interval says; that is the spread of the data, not of the average.
  3. "There is a 95% chance the true value is in this range.": the tempting one; the catch is that the interval has already been built, so the truth is either in it or not.

What makes an interval narrow, and what that is worth

  • More observations: width follows the square root of the sample size, so four times the people halves it.
  • Less variation between the things you measured: tighter data, tighter interval.
  • A lower confidence level: 90% is narrower than 99%, and buys that width with less cover.

Before trusting a narrow interval, ask how it was built. A narrow interval from a biased sample is a precise wrong answer.

Key terms

  • Confidence intervala range built from a sample, meant to cover the true value in a stated share of samples.
  • Confidence levelhow often the method covers the truth, not a chance for one interval.
  • Sample variabilityhow much a statistic moves from sample to sample; the thing the interval measures.
  • Margin of errorhalf the interval's width: the "±" a poll reports.
