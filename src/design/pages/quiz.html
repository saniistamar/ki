Q1

A 95% confidence interval built from a sample describes:

  • Athe range that holds 95% of the sample's values
  • Ba procedure that captures the true mean in 95% of samples
  • Cthe range where 95% of future samples will land
  • Dthe chance the sample mean equals the true mean

Q2

  • Bthe average delivery time is plausibly somewhere between 24 and 31 minutes
  • Cthe next delivery will arrive between 24 and 31 minutes
  • Dat least 95% of deliveries are inside that range

Q3

Q4

Q5

Q6

Q7

Answer key

Q1

B. The percentage belongs to the method: build intervals this way and 95% of them capture the true mean.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 4.

A is the commonest reading: it treats the interval as a range of the data. C and D move the probability onto the sample or onto the next draw.

Q2

B. The interval is about the average, computed from a sample, and it is plausible ground for the true average.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 6.

A and D confuse the spread of deliveries with the spread of the average; C turns a statement about the average into a prediction about one delivery.

Q3

A. More data narrows the interval; D widens it, and C widens it by asking for more certainty.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 7.

Q4

B. Width follows the square root of the sample size; four times the sample halves the width.

unverified — check this one.

The square-root relation is standard, but the slides state it only in words. Worth one line on the board before this quiz.

Q5

B. A larger sample's average wanders less from poll to poll, so the interval that surrounds it can be narrower.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 9.

A and C name things that were held fixed; D mistakes two different margins for one.

Q6

A. Width is set by variability between students and by the number asked, not by the mean itself.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 7, and the coursework example on p. 8.

Q7

B. The forecast is a probability about a single coming day; the interval reports how often the procedure captures the truth.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 4.

D reverses the two: the interval is built from the sample and speaks about the population.

Q8

B. Asking to be right more often costs width.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 7.

A is the certainty trap: more confidence, wider net. C and D treat the interval as a property of the data alone.

Q9

A strong answer: 95% describes the method: intervals built this way capture the true mean in 95% of samples. It is not a 95% chance for this one interval.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 4–5.

Look for the word "method" or "procedure", and for any sign that the student does not put the probability on the specific interval.

Q10

A strong answer: the interval halves, from ±3 to about ±1.5, because width tracks the square root of the sample size, so four times the shoppers, half the width.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 7.

Accept "roughly halves" with the square-root reason. A student who says "narrower, about half" without the reason gets part marks.

Q11

Any one of: "95% of deliveries take 24–31 minutes" (that is the spread of deliveries, not of the average); "the next delivery will fall in the range" (the interval is about the average); "95% of days the average is in the range" (it is about samples, not days).

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 4 and p. 6.

Q12

A strong answer contains: the interval claims the average spend rose, with the method capturing the truth 95% of the time; it does not claim every shopper spent 8% more, nor that the true rise is 14%; and before repeating 14%, ask how many shoppers were surveyed and how variable the spending was. A wide interval from a small sample says the number is shaky.

Source: your week-3 slides, p. 6 and p. 9; the sampling-variability discussion from week 2.

The last part separates recall from understanding: the size of the sample is what makes a wide interval trustworthy or not.
